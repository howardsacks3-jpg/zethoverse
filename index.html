01 / The scale of the unknown

There is a limit to what we know how to measure.

Look outward and the universe seems almost without end. Look inward and matter opens into atoms, particles and fields. Follow that journey far enough and we arrive at a remarkable scale: the Planck length.

≈ 1.616 × 10−35 metres

Important: Zeto length is our proposed concept, not an established scientific unit or discovery. Its value is an illustrative reference in the zeptometre range, not a confirmed fundamental length.

The Planck length is calculated from three fundamental constants: the speed of light, the reduced Planck constant and Newton's gravitational constant. It marks a scale where our existing descriptions of quantum physics and gravity are expected to need a deeper, unified account.

It is not a confirmed smallest possible length, and science has not established that space is made of tiny indivisible units. It is a signpost at the frontier of theory.

That distinction is where the mystery begins. We can calculate the scale. We can explore its implications mathematically. But we do not yet have a complete, experimentally confirmed theory describing what reality is like there.

02 / The science beneath the mystery

Three ideas. One extraordinary frontier.

Quantum physics

At very small scales, nature does not behave like the familiar world of everyday objects. Quantum theory describes probabilities, superposition and entanglement — effects tested repeatedly in laboratories.

Gravity and spacetime

Einstein's general relativity describes gravity as the geometry of spacetime. It works extraordinarily well on large scales, but combining it consistently with quantum theory remains one of physics' great open challenges.

The Planck frontier

When quantum effects and gravity may both become essential, familiar equations are expected to be incomplete. The Planck scale gives physicists a way to identify where a deeper theory may be needed.

The unanswered question

Is spacetime continuous? Does it have a deeper structure? Is there a meaningful notion of distance below the Planck scale? These are open questions, not settled discoveries.

03 / Experiments at the edge

How do we investigate something we cannot simply see?

Scientists do not need to photograph the smallest scales to test nature. They design experiments, measure outcomes with extraordinary precision and compare what happens with what competing theories predict.

Particle accelerators

At facilities such as CERN's Large Hadron Collider, particles are accelerated to high energies and brought into collision. Detectors study the particles and interactions produced. These experiments probe fundamental matter and forces — but they do not directly reach the Planck length.

Quantum interference and entanglement

Experiments with photons, atoms and other quantum systems test how probabilities combine, how interference appears and how entangled systems behave. They test the foundations of quantum mechanics with remarkable precision.

Gravitational-wave observatories

Instruments such as LIGO measure incredibly small changes in distance caused by passing gravitational waves. They reveal violent cosmic events and test general relativity in conditions far beyond ordinary laboratories. They are not microscopes for the Planck scale, but they let us test gravity in new ways.

Thought experiments

Physicists also use carefully constructed thought experiments to expose contradictions or limits in our assumptions. For example: what happens if we try to confine more and more energy into a smaller region? Quantum theory and gravity begin to pull the reasoning in different directions. Such questions help shape research, but a thought experiment is not itself an experimental discovery.

These are real research approaches with different aims. None has yet demonstrated a confirmed structure below the Planck length or proved the ZETHOVERSE concept.

04 / The dark universe

Most of the cosmos is still unexplained.

The mystery does not stop at the smallest scale. When astronomers weigh galaxies, map invisible mass and measure the expansion of space, the visible universe tells only part of the story.

Dark matter: the unseen gravity

Stars orbit the centres of galaxies at speeds that visible matter alone cannot adequately explain. Gravitational lensing bends light around concentrations of unseen mass. Galaxy clusters and the cosmic microwave background add further evidence. Together, these observations strongly support the existence of additional gravitating matter.

Dark matter does not shine like ordinary matter, but its gravity helps shape the cosmic web and the formation of galaxies. In that sense it helps hold galaxies and larger structures together. Scientists have not yet identified what dark matter is made of, and direct searches have not confirmed a dark-matter particle.

Dark energy: the expansion mystery

In 1998, observations of distant Type Ia supernovae revealed that the expansion of the universe is accelerating. The name dark energy describes the unknown cause of that acceleration. It is not simply another form of dark matter: dark energy is associated with the expansion of space, while dark matter adds gravitational attraction and helps structures grow.

Its physical nature remains unknown. It could be related to a cosmological constant, a changing field, or a deeper revision of our understanding of gravity. Observations continue to test these possibilities.

Where the two mysteries meet

Dark matter builds and shapes cosmic structure. Dark energy influences how the expansion of the universe changes the growth of that structure over time. By mapping galaxies, measuring gravitational lensing and studying the expansion history, researchers can test both parts of this cosmic picture together.

A new result about dark energy could therefore sharpen our understanding of how dark matter structures the universe. It would not, by itself, prove dark matter exists or identify its particles: the evidence for dark matter already comes from several independent gravitational observations.

The evidence for dark matter is strong but indirect; its underlying nature is unresolved. Dark energy is inferred from cosmic acceleration, but its cause is also unknown.

05 / Beyond established frameworks

Where does a theory end — and a new question begin?

String theory and its broader M-theory landscape are ambitious mathematical attempts to connect gravity with quantum physics. They have inspired deep research, but no string or M-theory model has yet received decisive experimental confirmation as a description of our universe.

That does not mean every idea within those frameworks is without merit, nor that they have been disproved. It means the decisive experimental bridge remains missing. Researchers continue to look for possible observational consequences and ways to constrain the theories.

The ZETHOVERSE question

ZETHOVERSE wants to explore an even more speculative horizon: whether the familiar language of particles, strings, fields and spacetime might itself be only one layer of a deeper reality.

We should be clear: this is a creative hypothesis and philosophical question, not a scientific theory that has surpassed string theory or M-theory. To become physics, it would need precise mathematics, predictions that differ from existing models, and observations or experiments capable of testing those predictions.

06 / Black holes, white holes and a universe within

Could a universe be born on the other side of a black hole?

Few objects challenge our understanding of reality like black holes. They are not science fiction: their gravitational effects have been measured, their shadows imaged, and collisions between them detected through gravitational waves. Yet what happens beyond an event horizon remains one of the deepest questions in physics.

Black holes: where space and time are transformed

A black hole forms when matter is compressed so intensely that, within its event horizon, nothing — not even light — can escape to the outside universe. The event horizon is a boundary, not a solid surface. General relativity predicts extraordinary conditions deeper inside, but our present theories are not expected to give the final word where quantum effects and gravity become inseparable.

At the centre of the simplest classical models lies a singularity: a sign that the mathematics has reached a limit, and that a deeper description may be needed. What truly replaces that description is still unknown.

White holes: the cosmic opposite?

Imagine reversing the picture. A theoretical white hole is a region from which matter and light can emerge, but into which nothing from the outside can enter. White holes appear in certain mathematical solutions related to Einstein's equations — sometimes described as the time-reversed counterpart of a black hole.

It is a captivating possibility: could a black hole and a white hole be connected through a deeper structure of spacetime? Some theoretical models explore black-to-white transitions or links to other regions of spacetime. But no white hole has ever been observed, and no evidence shows that an ordinary black hole opens into one.

What if our universe is inside a black hole?

Now take the question to its most extraordinary scale. Some speculative cosmological models ask whether a universe like ours could exist within the interior of a black hole formed in a larger, parent universe. In such ideas, what looks like a beginning from the inside might be connected to gravitational collapse from the outside.

This is not the accepted explanation for the Big Bang, and it is not an established description of our cosmos. There are major theoretical challenges, and current observations provide no compelling evidence that we live inside a black hole. But the question is scientifically intriguing because it asks whether black-hole physics, cosmic beginnings and the geometry of spacetime might be connected in ways we have not yet understood.

What if the universe we see as everything is, from somewhere else, inside something far greater?

That possibility should be treated as a question, not a conclusion. The challenge for science is to find mathematical models that make distinctive predictions — and observations that could prove them wrong. ZETHOVERSE is drawn to precisely this frontier: where a bold question meets the discipline of evidence.

07 / The holographic universe

Could the whole be reflected in the smallest part?

Imagine breaking a mirror. Each surviving shard can still reflect you: not because the whole mirror is physically hidden inside every fragment, but because each small surface can carry the light arriving from the scene. The reflection changes with the shard's angle, shape and size.

The mirror — and the hologram

Your broken-mirror image is a striking way into the question. A closer scientific analogy is a photographic hologram: unlike an ordinary photograph, a small piece of a holographic plate can still reconstruct an image of the whole scene, although the image is usually less sharp and has a narrower viewing angle.

That does not mean every fragment contains a tiny physical copy of the object. It means information about the scene is distributed through the recorded interference pattern.

The holographic principle: information on a boundary

In theoretical physics, the holographic principle grew from the surprising thermodynamics of black holes. A black hole's entropy is proportional to the area of its event horizon, rather than the volume inside it. This suggested that the information capacity of a region of space may be described in terms of degrees of freedom associated with a lower-dimensional boundary.

The best-developed example is the AdS/CFT correspondence, a powerful mathematical relationship between certain theories with gravity in a higher-dimensional space and quantum theories defined on a lower-dimensional boundary. It gives researchers a concrete setting in which holographic ideas can be studied.

The ZETHOVERSE question: does every part carry a reflection of the whole?

Now take the metaphor one step further. What if the universe is not merely described by information on a distant boundary? What if every tiny region carries some relational imprint of the wider whole — like a shard that still reflects the scene from its own position?

This is an imaginative extension, not what the established holographic principle currently claims. Physics does not show that every particle, point or fragment contains a complete copy of the universe. The challenge would be to define precisely what information is encoded, how it is shared, and what measurable prediction would distinguish this idea from existing theories.

If a fragment can reflect the whole, how much of the universe might be written into the smallest part?

Perhaps the most intriguing possibility is not that every fragment contains everything in miniature, but that no part is entirely isolated: each may be defined by relationships that connect it to a larger structure. That is a philosophical and scientific question worth exploring — with imagination guided by evidence.

08 / The zeptoverse — a new experimental window

Knocking on the door of the zeptoverse.

In November 2026, reports described a new step in particle physics: researchers using data from the LHCb experiment at CERN investigated an extraordinarily rare decay of a particle containing a beauty quark. The work opens a way to test whether unknown particles or forces might leave subtle fingerprints in the behaviour of familiar matter.

A real particle process

The decay under study is the neutral B meson decaying into a pair of muons:

B0 → μ+ + μ−

A B0 meson is a short-lived composite particle containing a beauty (bottom) quark and a down antiquark. A muon is a heavier relative of the electron; the two muons produced in this decay have opposite electric charge. This decay is exceptionally rare, which makes it a sensitive place to look for small deviations from the Standard Model of particle physics.

What does “zeptoverse” mean?

A zeptometre is 10−21 metres — one sextillionth of a metre. The term “zeptoverse” is an informal nickname for the extraordinarily short-distance frontier that researchers hope to investigate through high-energy collisions and precision measurements.

It is important to understand what this scale means. Scientists do not take a photograph of a zeptometre-sized object, nor does the experiment reveal a tiny universe inside a proton. At these energies, measurements of particle behaviour can test interactions associated with extremely short distance scales. The scale is inferred through the physics, not directly imaged.

The zeptometre is still enormously larger than the Planck length, approximately 1.616 × 10−35 metres — about ten trillion times larger. The Planck length remains a theoretical frontier, not a scale reached by the LHC.

How can an unseen particle leave a fingerprint?

In quantum physics, a particle that is too heavy to be produced directly can still influence a process through quantum effects. If an unknown particle or force contributes to a rare decay, it could change how often that decay happens or alter its measured properties.

Researchers compare the measured decay with the Standard Model prediction. A reliable, repeatable difference could point to new physics. The reported result is an important experimental step, not proof that a new particle, force or dark-matter candidate has been discovered. Evidence must become stronger and be tested against further data and independent measurements.

What this does — and does not — tell us

What it does: demonstrates how rare particle decays can be used to investigate possible new physics at distance scales far beyond ordinary intuition.

What it does not: establish that quarks contain smaller objects, reveal a miniature universe, demonstrate structure below the Planck length, or prove that dark matter has been identified.

Earlier collider searches for quark substructure and this LHCb study address different questions. A search finding no evidence for quark constituents at its tested scale does not rule out every possible new particle or interaction that could influence a different process.

The ZETHOVERSE connection: the fractured mirror

Here the scientific result meets a question that inspires ZETHOVERSE. Could the smallest constituents of matter be connected to a deeper architecture of reality? Could a tiny part carry not a physical copy of the whole universe, but some information or relational imprint of the larger structure?

The LHCb result does not demonstrate this. It gives us a real example of how scientists search for hidden layers: not by assuming they exist, but by identifying precise measurements where an unknown layer might leave a detectable trace.

Our “Fractured Mirror Hypothesis” is therefore a speculative question, not a discovery: might the smallest parts of reality retain relationships or information connecting them to the wider universe? To become a scientific hypothesis, it would need a mathematical definition, a prediction that differs from existing physics, and a way to test that prediction.

What if the smallest scales do not contain a miniature universe — but hold clues to how the whole universe is connected?
